Ben, maybe talk a little bit about going from revolutionary justice to more boring justice with laws and statutes.

**Ben:** Yeah, that transition really applies at least aspirationally to the Soviet Union in the 1930s. As strange as it may sound, the minister of justice under **Stalin**, a guy named **Andrei Vyshynski**, was really the architect of the infamous **show trials** in **Moscow**.

These trials involved some of the most famous and renowned old Bolsheviks, including **Nikolai Bukharin**, people who had been very close to **Lenin** and members of the party long before the Bolshevik Revolution in 1917. They appeared on witness stands and confessed to the most outlandish crimes, such as:

```markdown
- spying for Great Britain
- spying for Nazi Germany
- spying for Japan
```

Sometimes all three simultaneously. Once you get that detached from reality, it doesn't really matter. Of course, they had almost all been tortured or had their family members threatened. Their confessions were scripted in advance of the trials. These are the hallmarks of a **show trial**.

But the irony is that **Vyshynski** was also the person who essentially oversaw the transition away from, as you put it, revolutionary justice towards something more professional, which he called **socialist legality**.

**Revolutionary justice** is the idea that you don't need professionally trained lawyers, as bourgeois societies choose to place much of the legal decision-making process in the hands of people who have degrees and pedigrees, who have passed through elite, usually conservative, educational institutions.

Revolutionary justice is the idea that real justice, capital J justice, flows most profoundly and reliably from the instinct of people who are on the right side of history, that is, workers or members of the working class.

So you don't need professional jurists. What you need are workers whose gut instincts about right and wrong are the most reliable and some people have said infallible way to decide guilt or innocence.

In the 1920s, which was the most revolutionary period of Soviet history, when all kinds of experiments...
**Revolutionary justice** was being carried out, some of it just **breathtaking**, others absolutely **horrifying**. This was the period when revolutionary justice was seen as the highest form of adjudicating issues in courts. Everything from **divorce cases** to questions of **political justice**, both **high and low**, and everything in between, were subjected to this system.

But what the **Bolsheviks** soon learned was that revolutionary justice was really **unpredictable**. Workers did not always produce the results that the party leadership wanted or expected. As in so many other arenas, by the 1930s, the Soviet leadership was **retrenching** in how justice should work. They decided it would be a good idea to have **professionally trained judges**, people who could maintain certain standards of **precedence**, the use of evidence, and what a confession should look like.

The actual practice of justice was nothing like what we would call **professional**. These **show trials** were travesties of justice according to Western standards. But we have to bear in mind, and I think **Ian** will probably be able to speak to this in the **Chinese case**, that the term show trial is often used with condescension—implying “this is all just pretend,” “this is a bullshit trial,” “not real justice being meted out,” and that everything was **scripted in advance**. 

The Soviets themselves used the term, because that’s how the trials were practiced. But the word really means **demonstrative trial**—a trial with a **pedagogical goal**: to teach the population about right and wrong, and above all, **the power of the state to punish guilty parties**.

Once we move away from the condescension that the term show trial conjures up, we enter a more complicated terrain. Western legal systems are also engaged in the business of teaching, which is why trials are **public**. It’s not just so that the actions of the prosecution, defense, judges, and sometimes the jury can be scrutinized. It’s because trials also function as **classrooms** where lessons about right and wrong are broadcast and where **state power is on display**.

It becomes much more interesting when you realize that **Vyshynskiy** was presiding over a transition away from so-called revolutionary justice. It wasn’t just about these farcical show trials. It was also about creating a Soviet version of a **professional judiciary**.
