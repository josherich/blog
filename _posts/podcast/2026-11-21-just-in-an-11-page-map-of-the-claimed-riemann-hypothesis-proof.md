[Just in: An 11-page map of the claimed Riemann Hypothesis proof](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YxMoQ76HgtA)

Until yesterday evening, the proof of the Riemann hypothesis announced by **Levent Alpöge** existed in two forms, a four-page note and seventeen million lines of Lean, and neither of them let a mathematician see how the argument goes. A third document has now appeared. It is an eleven-page guide to the architecture of the proof, published in a repository that belongs to the mathematician **Bartosz Naskręcki**, and it was produced with the help of an AI system by reading the Lean code and the note.

It is not the author's own account, and its later sections report what the code says without deriving it again. With that caution in mind, it is the first readable map of the route.

The note describes the argument as a descendant of a proof by **Stepanov**. For curves over a finite field, the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis was proved by **Weil** in the nineteen forties, and in nineteen sixty-nine Stepanov found an elementary proof. He constructs an auxiliary function that vanishes to high order at the points of the curve, and since a function cannot have more zeros than its degree allows, this bounds the number of points from above. Upper bounds over all the finite extensions of the field then turn out to be enough.

The announced proof looks for a counterpart over the integers to each of these four ingredients, and the hardest one to find is the multiplicity.

The first step is a reduction. Because of the functional equation, a zero to the left of the critical line would produce one to the right of it, so it is enough to show that there are no zeros with real part greater than one half.

The proof then works with a single function of a real variable y, which I will call **G**. By definition, G of y adds up the logarithms of the primes over all the prime powers n up to y, each one weighted by y over n, minus one, and then subtracts the expected main term. The weight is a ramp that falls to zero at n equals y, and the reason for choosing a ramp over a sharp cut-off is that the smoothing makes the sum over the zeros converge absolutely.

That sum is the explicit formula, which is a property of G and not part of its definition. It says that G of y equals the logarithm of two pi, minus a sum over the zeros rho of y to the power rho, divided by rho times rho minus one. If every zero has real part one half, each term has the size of the square root of y, and the whole sum is at most zero point zero four six times the square root of y. A zero further to the right, with real part beta, would contribute a term of size y to the power beta. When the function is computed up to ten million, it stays well inside that band.

The next step explains why the proof only ever needs to bound G from above. A theorem of **Landau** says that when a function keeps a constant sign, its Mellin transform has a singularity at the real point on the edge of its region of convergence. Applied to a constant times y to the power theta, minus G, it shows that a bound of that kind for all large y would exclude every zero with real part greater than theta. The heuristic is that all the zeros appear in the transform of G as poles with the same sign, so they cannot cancel one another, and a single zero cannot hide.

The arithmetic in this proof delivers the bound only on certain intervals, which the guide calls **windows**, running from Y to Y to the power three halves. The theorem is therefore proved in a localized form, in which a zero to the right of theta forces G above y to the power theta somewhere inside every sufficiently late window.

Everything that remains is contained in one statement, which says that for arbitrarily large Y the function is at most the square root of x, times a small power of x, at every point x of the window.

The idea behind that estimate can be seen in a classical example. The factorial of a is divisible by each prime p to a power close to a over p, plus a over p squared, and so on,
And taking logarithms turns this into a sum over prime powers with a weight very much like the ramp. The other side of the equation is the logarithm of a factorial, which Stirling's formula computes.

The simplest case in the guide replaces the factorial with the derivatives at the origin of a theta function on an elliptic curve with complex multiplication, divided by powers of its period. According to the note, these numbers are algebraic integers, and they are divisible by high powers of the primes that stay inert in the quadratic field.

A non-zero algebraic integer that is divisible by many primes cannot be small, by the product formula, and so the divisibility, which is a prime sum with a ramp, is bounded above by the logarithm of the size of the number, which can be computed. The computation produces a main term that begins with a times the logarithm of a minus one minus Euler's constant, and this is the same centring that appears in the definition of G.

A single derivative gives error terms that are far too large, and this is where multiplicity comes in. The derivative is replaced by a chain of m theta lifts, which are linear maps between spaces of automorphic forms on unitary groups of growing dimension, defined over a field that climbs a cyclotomic tower as x grows.

The product formula is replaced by an inequality of **Bost** on the slopes of lattices, which says that whatever the chain gains in divisibility at the primes, it has to pay for in its norm at the infinite places. According to the guide, the errors come out as x over m, plus m, plus the square root of x. The first term is the error of the whole chain shared among its lifts, and the second is a cost that each lift incurs as the chain grows longer, so the best choice is m of the order of the square root of x. That choice is the step that the note attributes to **Claude**.

Three further pieces complete the route, and I will only name them here. The bound arrives for a combination of all the characters of the Galois group of the field, so an average with positive weights is used to keep the characters that are wanted, and two auxiliary primes are chosen to remove the others.

The divisibility holds only up to a defect measured by Galois cohomology, which is controlled through Iwasawa theory and a theorem of **Ferrero and Washington**. That control is available only at certain scales, which is the reason the analytic theorem had to work on windows. Both of these rest on the construction of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms over these fields, which the archive proves formally for itself.

Two different questions should be kept apart, and the first is whether the formal proof is valid. The complete **Lean** proof has now been accepted by two independently written kernels, and its five final theorems, the **Riemann hypothesis** among them, match the statements in **Mathlib**. The guide also reports a separate machine check of the last step, which leads from the final estimate to the Riemann hypothesis.

The second question is whether the guide's account of how the proof works is accurate, and the answer depends on the part. The classical part, which covers the function G, the explicit formula and Landau's theorem, is textbook analysis, and its formulas and the computation up to ten million have been rechecked independently of the guide.

The arithmetic part, with the theta lifts, the slopes and the Iwasawa theory, was read from the Lean code with the help of an AI system, and no specialist has refereed that reading yet. **Alpöge** has said that he intends to write the argument up properly, and that account, when it comes, is the one this map should be tested against.
