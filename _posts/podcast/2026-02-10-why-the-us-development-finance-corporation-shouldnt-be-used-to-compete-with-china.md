Today, we're going to return to the topic of **international development, finance, and international investment**. We talked last week with **Christophe Nedepel** about the huge jump in Chinese **BRI engagements overseas in 2025** — $128 billion in new construction contracts and $85 billion in investments. So about **$200 billion of overall activity**, breaking records across the board, really kind of putting an end to the narrative that the **BRI is dead** or the **BRI is spent**. And that is apparently not the case.

Today, we're going to look at the **U.S. side of the equation**. Last month in Washington, the **Development Finance Corporation (DFC)** finally got its six-year authorization, and that took a long time. Pretty much most of last year, they were debating this **$205 billion investment cap**. That will, again, be over six years.

They have a **$5 billion equity revolving fund**, increased **equity investment authority**, up to **40% minority ownership**. What this is all kind of speaking to is that the **Development Finance Corporation** was intended to invest in the areas that the private sector wouldn't go or that they could supplement something. But they were very, very limited in where they could invest in the early stages of DFC. 

So Congress now has widened a lot, making it easier for the U.S. DFC to go invest in countries and in projects that otherwise they wouldn't be able to do. This includes:

- Increased investment authority to cover all international geographies other than the wealthiest 20 countries.  
- Expanded equity investment capacity.

That was a big limitation early on. Now, when the **DFC was founded in 2019**, countering **China** was a very big motivation for Congress to save what was then called the **Overseas Private Investment Corporation (OPIC)** from the chopping block. Today, that **China theme remains very, very important** in justifying DFC's existence. That was very much a theme throughout the reauthorization hearings that we heard last summer.

Let me give you a taste of it from Democratic Rhode Island Representative **Gabe Amo** during one of those sessions last year:

> "As others have mentioned, the Development Finance Corporation provides enormous return on investment for the American taxpayer. By working with the private sector to build development projects, whether it's strengthening mineral supply chains in **Angola** or expanding fish farms in **Vietnam**,  
> the DFC advances the United States' foreign policy goals and our economic interests.  
> The DFC opens up markets to American businesses, thereby reducing the reliance on foreign aid and shifting our relationship toward an equal playing field where American investment advances American diplomacy.  
> In doing so, it's the perfect counter to China's **Belt and Road Initiative**,  
> a coercive financing scheme that pressures countries into accepting unfavorable investment terms while forcing them to look the other way on China's human rights abuses."

That is certainly a take on **Capitol Hill** about the Belt and Road that is not widely shared among many in the Global South. Nonetheless, we'll save that discussion for another day.

But all of this discussion about China and the DFC prompted the folks over at the **Quincy Institute** to publish a fascinating article that came out again in December, about the time around when it was reauthorized: 

**U.S. capital investments for something other than beating China**, that was published on the **Responsible Statecraft website**, which is run by the Quincy Institute, by **Karthik Shankaran**, who is a senior research fellow in geoeconomics in the Global South program at the Quincy Institute, and his colleague **Dan Ford**, a junior research fellow at the Quincy Institute's Global South program.

Dan and Karthik join me today from Washington and the West Coast in California. A very good morning to both of you.

**Good morning to you, Eric. Thank you for having us.**

Before we get into your article, I want you to give maybe our audience a little bit of the understanding of the role that China played in the discourse, going all the way back to the OPIC days that was the precursor to DFC, and kind of bring us up to the present in terms of that role — that very prominent role that China has played in the thinking of DFC and the rationale for it.
