---

It's easy to forget that, not long ago, the US was actually China’s biggest ally. In the early 2000s, Washington viewed Beijing as a crucial counterbalance against Moscow and consistently supported its integration into the global economy since the rapprochement of the 1970s, including China’s entry into the World Trade Organization. Today, however, the relationship is markedly different. So, what led to this rapid change? When did the shift occur, and can we now consider American rapprochement a strategic error?

---

Bob Davis is a former senior editor at the *Wall Street Journal*, who was posted in China from 2011 to 2014. In recent years, he has conducted a comprehensive series of interviews with high-level officials from various American administrations for the online magazine *The Wire China*. He has spoken with defense secretaries, ambassadors, national security advisers, treasury secretaries, and more. These interviews have now been compiled into a new e-book titled *Broken Engagement*. They offer insight into the evolving dynamics of US-China relations as seen through the perspectives of those directly involved in shaping America's policies. Bob joins us on the podcast now to share his insights. Welcome to *Chinese Whispers*, Bob Davis.

---

Thank you for having me.

---

So, can you tell me about the interviews you've conducted? What is the aim of your project with *The Wire China*?

---

Over the past two years, I’ve interviewed both former and current officials from administrations beginning with the first Bush administration, spanning over 40 years. The intent was to trace the trajectory of the US-China relationship during its periods of improvement and decline, and the transition into the hostile environment we see today. I wanted to highlight the major turning points, key players, and their thoughts at those times by going back to them with a series of questions.

---

This gives you a fascinating overview of US-China relations over these decades. I found it particularly interesting that you consistently asked your interviewees whether they believed a prosperous China is in America's interest. You’ve tracked how their answers to that question have changed over the years. In the earlier years, the consensus was a resounding yes.

---

Yes, that’s true. It was indeed a yes until the Trump administration arrived. The prevailing belief was that a wealthier China would open up as a larger export market, create more opportunities for US investment, and ultimately lead to more American jobs. Additionally, while not a military ally, China was seen as a country we could rely on during emergencies, contributing to a long list of potential benefits. There was even an underlying hope that improved engagement might foster more freedoms for individual Chinese citizens on the mainland.

---

But primarily, was this engagement driven by national security concerns rather than a desire to reform China?

---

Absolutely. In the beginning, it was all about national security. If you look back at the first Bush administration, China was a minor economic player but always an important military concern. After all, the US and China had engaged in a proxy war in Korea that ended in a stalemate. Thus, while China was a point of contention, the aim was to engage it more closely to drive a wedge between China and Russia. 

---

Right, and as noted in your upcoming book, which compiles these interviews, even the Tiananmen Square protests and the subsequent crackdown didn't significantly alter that agenda. However, you point to 2016 as a critical turning point; clearly, things are different now. Why do you identify 2016 as pivotal?

---

It truly was a turning point. When Trump took office, he campaigned against China, criticizing it for "raping" the US economically. He brought into his administration numerous individuals who viewed China as America’s foremost threat. The seeds of this shift didn't solely plant with Trump; towards the end of the Obama administration, there was growing sentiment that the US had engaged too closely with China and hadn’t pushed back hard enough. For example, there were several attempts to acquire US semiconductor companies that the US blocked.

---

But Trump marked a substantial departure. And on the other side, China under Xi Jinping was also a different beast than his predecessors—more aggressive and self-perceiving as a major global force. So we had both Xi Jinping consolidating power and Trump labeling China not merely a competitor but an adversary. I think COVID really served as a catalyst for escalating tensions.

---

Indeed. At the start of the Trump administration, despite the hawkish rhetoric and his tough-talking cabinet, the first action he took was to sign a trade deal, which is a conventional move for any American president engaging with China, dating back to the Clinton era, if not earlier. However, COVID altered the entire dynamic. Trump blamed the outbreak for affecting his re-election prospects—and with good reason. Regardless of opinions on the virus's origins, it undeniably spread from China to the world. Some may argue we’re in “Trump two” during the pandemic, while I believe we’re now seeing “Trump three,” which represents a slightly different version of the same individual.

---

I want to dive deeper into Trump shortly, but let’s focus on China for a moment. When did the American foreign policy establishment, as you've interacted with them, begin to recognize that Xi was a different type of leader?

---

It took a while—a couple of years, in fact. During my time in China from 2011 to 2014, there was a crucial meeting around 2012 or 2013 called the Third Plenary Session. In that meeting, the Chinese laid out their economic plans, clearly emphasizing reliance on market forces.

---

Wasn’t there also a strong assertion of the Communist Party's leading role in that document?

---

Yes, but many overlooked that aspect, focusing instead on the government’s signals indicating reform intentions. Xi Jinping had previously governed coastal provinces that actively encouraged foreign investment. His history led many to perceive him as someone who valued markets and was comfortable with foreigners, especially given that his family had faced persecution during the Cultural Revolution. It took a few years for observers to realize he was not the kind of reform-minded leader many had hoped for.

---

I found Gary Locke, the former ambassador to China under Obama, quite enlightening in your discussions, particularly about how the West misread Xi—initial impressions depicted him as somewhat liberal.

---

Certainly. There’s also Hank Paulson, who served as Treasury Secretary under George W. Bush. He had extensive experience with China and was completely misled, viewing Xi as a reformer, which he ultimately isn’t; he represents a different style of leadership altogether.

---

I often wonder, as we veer slightly off topic, whether perceptions in the West about reformers in authoritarian systems are often a projection of our own democratic ideals onto those leaders—essentially a form of wishful thinking.

---

I wholeheartedly agree. During the Clinton administration, for instance, when China was seeking to join the World Trade Organization, the Chinese president was Zhu Rongji—a clear economic reformer who utilized the WTO as a way to push through painful reforms domestically. Yet, those reforms resulted in significant unpopularity for him, as many state-owned enterprises were shuttered, throwing millions out of work. 

---

For years, people hunted for the next Zhu Rongji, including during the Trump era. The Chinese government adeptly presented figures that resonated with the West—leaders who spoke fluent English, were educated in top Western institutions, and seemed to champion reform. This didn’t necessarily indicate they were political reformers desiring increased democracy, although some might have.

---

And then, of course, there was the pivotal moment when Xi Jinping abolished term limits for the presidency.

---

Yes, indeed. If anyone missed the signal before that, which they shouldn’t have, it was unmistakable then. How did this move resonate among your interviewees as they reflected on that time?

---

At that moment, it wasn’t too surprising. Xi had been accruing more power for quite some time. Remember, being president is almost a ceremonial role.

---

Exactly; it's akin to being the Queen of England. The role of the Communist Party leader, however, has never had term limits. Nevertheless, one of Deng Xiaoping's significant reforms was introducing term limits—an unusual feature in an autocratic system. 

---

The challenge with autocratic systems is that when a singular leader is in place who makes mistakes, no one wants to challenge them, leading to a downward spiral.

---

For our listeners who may not be familiar with the distinctions, could you clarify the difference between the roles of General Secretary and President in China, especially given that Xi holds both titles? 

---

Sure. Xi holds both positions, yet his real power primarily stems from his role as General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party. The presidency, while having now been stripped of term limits, is more of a head of state role than the true source of his authority.

---

Speaking of Deng, several interviewees noted that Deng Xiaoping had a more measured approach to dealing with foreigners, encapsulated in his motto of "hiding your strength, biding your time." They felt Xi revealed his strategy too early and alarmed the American administration, especially during Trump’s tenure. How do you view that perspective?

---

I think that assessment is spot-on. One of the most intriguing insights I gathered was from Robert Gates, who served as CIA Director under a Republican administration and later as Defense Secretary under a Democratic one—certainly one of the most realistic pragmatists in American politics. He argued that engagement might still have been effective, suggesting that Xi was somewhat rattled, perceiving the West’s economic embrace as indeed having an impact on China. One could speculate that this motivation spurred Xi’s extensive anti-corruption campaign as a means to purify the party and align it more closely with its foundational values.
---

**Cindy Yu:** So, that leads me quite nicely, Bob, to my next question. There's an ongoing debate among scholars and analysts about how much of the changes happening in China can be attributed to Xi Jinping as an individual leader versus the overall system, the structure of Chinese politics, and the global forces at play. 

**Bob Davis:** Right. The question is: could another leader have reacted similarly, or could the party as a whole have had concerns—essentially “freaked out”—and taken China down this path even without him? Some argue that he is not the origin of China's tighter control; rather, he's merely an actor in a long-running trend. I don't believe anything in history is inevitable.

---

**Cindy Yu:** Before Xi, we had Hu Jintao, a weaker leader under a system of collective leadership, established by Deng Xiaoping. Do you think it was inevitable that the next leader would be much more solitary in their rule?

**Bob Davis:** I don’t think that was inevitable. Xi still has a standing committee of six, including himself, which serves as the ruling body. However, he clearly dominates it and places his allies in key positions. It's true that the leadership was completely upended and, yes, they were entirely freaked out by Glasnost. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** It sounds like the leadership has always been worried about any signs of independence.

**Bob Davis:** Absolutely. I remember doing a story about a city in China called Kaifeng, where a small community descends from Jewish merchants who arrived a thousand years ago and consider themselves Jewish. By the time I left, the government cracked down on that group. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** That's surprising! They think everyone in the world has moved to China?

**Bob Davis:** Exactly. Anything that isn't under their control seems to trigger alarm. Glasnost certainly affected them, as did Tiananmen. If the troops hadn’t fired on the protesters, that government might have fallen. They never want to be in a position where their control relies on the loyalty of recruits to suppress popular sentiment. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** Considering this history, what lessons should we take from that turning point? Looking at the changes since 2016, who should have seen them coming? 

**Bob Davis:** Well, it's clear that it took too long for the U.S. to recognize the changes in China. Some people who were in the Obama administration, and later in Biden's, expressed that they should have acted more forcefully much earlier regarding the South China Sea. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** Can you elaborate on that important moment during Obama's presidency?

**Bob Davis:** Certainly! A significant moment was in 2015 when Xi Jinping was in the Rose Garden with President Obama. He promised not to militarize the South China Sea and to stop cyber spying on companies for commercial gain. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** What did Mike Rogers, the former head of the National Security Agency, say about that assurance?

**Bob Davis:** He noted that the Chinese backed off on cyber spying for six months—that's it. A public promise made in front of the president lasted only six months. When I asked Rogers about Obama's response, he suggested that Obama believed back-door negotiations would work better. While diplomatic, Rogers clearly thought a more decisive approach was needed.

---

**Cindy Yu:** Shifting gears, let’s talk about the Trump presidency. How do you compare his China policy in this term with that of Biden or his first term?

**Bob Davis:** Biden's policy largely continues the approach of Trump, though he seeks to work with allies more effectively. However, relations were so strained that their main aim was to keep both nations from going to war, which is the worst possible outcome. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** How did Trump’s aims differ in his first administration versus his current one?

**Bob Davis:** In Trump's first term, China was clearly the target; however, in this term, it seems like everyone is a target. Trump views the world economically; any country with a large, persistent trade surplus is seen as taking advantage of the U.S. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** Do you think he might be looking to work with Xi Jinping instead?

**Bob Davis:** It's possible. Trump has always believed he can cut a deal. In this current administration, he seems to have yes-men around him, which could lead him to underestimate the complexities involved. However, he also has some very hawkish advisors on China.

---

**Cindy Yu:** It's still early in this administration, but there have been significant developments already.

**Bob Davis:** Yes, we’re still figuring things out. It’s only been a couple of months since we started this conversation. There may be different factions emerging, just like in the first Trump administration. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** There’s been a discussion about a "reverse Kissinger" approach, which I believe was mentioned by some of your interviewees. Can you clarify that for our listeners?

**Bob Davis:** Of course! In the same way that Henry Kissinger worked to separate China from Russia to bring China into the Western orbit, some in the MAGA camp are considering this reverse strategy. For instance, I interviewed Robert O’Brien, Trump’s former national security adviser, who noted they contemplated approaching the Russians to remind them that China may seek to reclaim territory lost historically.

---

**Cindy Yu:** How far did that initiative go?

**Bob Davis:** It didn’t go very far, largely because of the Russia-Ukraine war. However, there’s more public discourse about it now than there was before. Some former Biden officials and China experts think it's worth exploring if it could lead to a resolution between Russia and Ukraine. 

---

**Cindy Yu:** It seems like the relationship between China and Russia has changed drastically, making this notion of a split much less feasible.

**Bob Davis:** You're right. Currently, during the war, China has provided significant economic support to Russia. There doesn’t appear to be a split, and both Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin understand the complexities of their positions. When they interact with Trump, you often see them publicly reinforcing their ties to one another.
**Bob Davis:** Yeah, Cindy, I agree with those in the Biden administration. The historical parallels we’re looking at today don’t quite support the idea that a rapprochement with Russia can bring an end to this competition. 

With China being perceived as the new USSR, there’s something Robert O'Brien said that really stood out to me—this line of reasoning surrounding territory. In their narrative of the century of humiliation, the Chinese don’t emphasize the Russian territorial aspects very much. The fact that Vladivostok was once a Chinese city is a story that barely gets mentioned. 

Nationally, those anniversaries don't receive significant attention, though people in northeastern China may still feel the historical weight more acutely. The next chapter of humiliation seems more focused on the Americans, the Brits, and the West in general, rather than on Russia. It makes me wonder how well U.S. officials read the Chinese situation domestically. 

I'm not just talking about the Trump administration here; I mean across the board with U.S. officials you’ve spoken to. Do they really grasp how the Chinese perceive the world and their own identity?

**Cindy Yu:** That’s a great question. 

**Bob Davis:** Kirk Campbell, who was the main China expert in the Biden administration, has worked on China issues since the Clinton era. He pointed out a critical flaw in the U.S. government—it’s a lack of knowledge and information about China. He believes they don’t adequately understand China. 

To your original question: should they have recognized earlier that Xi Jinping was a different type of leader? Yes, they clearly should have. This is also an issue for China. U.S. history shows similar misunderstandings; you can look back at the belief that Saddam Hussein possessed weapons of mass destruction. That turned out to be a monumental foreign policy blunder. 

**Cindy Yu:** Absolutely. 

**Bob Davis:** Likewise, Saddam misread U.S. intentions regarding its willingness to defend Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. Misunderstandings like these are not uncommon; they can prove to be quite dangerous. With U.S. focus so heavily centered on the Middle East and its history with the Soviet Union, attempting to pivot toward Asia has proven quite challenging.

I’ve always been intrigued by Chinese foreign policy: are they exceptionally strategic, or are they just extraordinarily fortunate? Yes, the issues China raises should indeed be concerning. For example, when China cut off imports from Lithuania simply because of their actions involving Taiwan—that is troubling. 

Or when they exert pressure on Australia due to dissent over COVID-related matters—that’s clear economic coercion. Yet, on the other hand, we have Russia mobilizing 100,000 troops along the Ukrainian border. Where does your attention go in such scenarios? 

**Cindy Yu:** Right, that’s a good point. 

**Bob Davis:** The Chinese manage to remain in the news without overtaking the top stories. An invasion of Taiwan would certainly make waves, but so far, it hasn’t escalated to that point. Similarly, while occurrences like ramming boats in the Philippines are severe, they haven’t instigated a full invasion.

It seems like they engage just enough to test the limits of what can be tolerated, progressing in a gray area rather than crossing into outright invasion. With regards to Taiwan, it’s unlikely we would see an invasion, but a gradual encroachment into what’s deemed acceptable is probably the smarter route for them.

**Cindy Yu:** That is indeed a clever strategy. 

**Bob Davis:** Whether they are lucky or smart, they’ve navigated their relations with the West skillfully, although the current state of affairs between China and the West is deteriorating—a reality that is harming China economically. 

**Cindy Yu:** Exactly. 

**Bob Davis:** One has to question whether the wolf warrior diplomacy is as clever as it seems. Many observers agree that Taiwan has been too complacent in its own defense strategy.

**Cindy Yu:** Can you elaborate on that perspective?

**Bob Davis:** Yes, there’s a widespread sentiment on this issue—across both Republican and Democrat lines—that Taiwan is a primary target for China. With a population of 23 to 24 million, Taiwan is a leader in semiconductor manufacturing and has a highly skilled workforce, so its importance cannot be overstated.

Historically, one might consider the situation in Israel, which constantly feels threatened and has developed strong missile defense systems in response. You'd expect Taiwan to do something similar, given they face threats from missiles and naval forces. However, it seems they haven’t prioritized their defense capabilities as they should. 

Moreover, the conscription system in Taiwan appears to fall short—six months of training doesn’t seem adequate for adequately preparing a defense force. It’s a tall order to expect the United States Navy to step in to assist and potentially risk losing thousands of personnel when Taiwan isn’t doing its part for its own defense.

**Cindy Yu:** That’s a crucial issue. 

**Bob Davis:** It is highly frustrating for U.S. military planners. While everyone desires to protect Taiwan's semiconductor manufacturing assets, if the drumbeat of war becomes a reality, the nation itself must primarily assume responsibility for its defense. 

**Cindy Yu:** Fascinatingly, this frustration has persisted through various administrations. Given all of these insights you’ve gathered from your interviews, do you believe there are lessons to learn? Has the U.S. ever been able to compel China to act against its own interests?

**Bob Davis:** There may be some degree of influence. Regarding human rights, China is clearly not a democracy, and it seems unlikely to become one. Yet, people in China enjoy more individual freedoms than they did during Deng Xiaoping’s time. For instance, they have the freedom to choose whom to marry and what occupations to pursue. 

While poorer migrants who move to cities often lack proper identification documentation and face challenges, they typically aren’t arrested—indicating an increase in individual freedoms. Engagement with the West likely played a role in this development. 

China's engagement has also diminished its missile proliferation activities compared to the past, suggesting the benefits of interaction. I believe that influencing China effectively requires a united front with allies. Their economic vitality hinges on exports and investment, so collective action from allies could make a significant difference. 

However, one must accept that no nation will undermine its own interests. Trump’s approach will be interesting to watch, as it revolves around leveraging tariffs to compel countries to follow U.S. directives. 

**Cindy Yu:** That’s a valid point. 

**Bob Davis:** China stands apart from other countries like Mexico, which is highly dependent on the U.S. Despite complexities in their relationship with America, it's fair to say Mexico’s situation isn’t similar to China’s strong geopolitical standing. 

Finding a way to align with what China views as advantageous remains a challenge. 

**Cindy Yu:** The book wraps up with reflections from Biden administration officials. Are you planning to interview people from the new Trump presidency as well?

**Bob Davis:** I’ll certainly try to do that. It’s challenging since a new administration doesn’t always have a clearly defined China policy. I hope some former senior Biden officials might be willing to share their insights now that they are out of office.

**Cindy Yu:** That sounds promising! 

**Bob Davis:** If I can connect with others in similar positions, it might facilitate further discussions.

**Bob Davis:** Thank you for having me, and I appreciate the opportunity to discuss these topics.
