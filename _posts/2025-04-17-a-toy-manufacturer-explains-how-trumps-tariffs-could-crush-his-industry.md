While some toy products can indeed be made in Vietnam and Indonesia, the people I know in the toy business seem to fall into three categories. The first group thinks, "I’m going to cheat to win." The second group is scrambling to shift manufacturing to Southeast Asia as quickly as possible, which includes us. The third group believes the policy doesn’t make sense and is taking a wait-and-see approach. 

**Molson Hart**: The enforcement of these policies will determine if the first group faces trouble. If tariffs are reversed, groups two and three might either thrive or falter. Generally, people predict tariffs will lead to inflation, pushing toy prices higher, but they could just as easily result in deflation. Companies that had budgets of $300,000 or $500,000 for inventory now face 145% tariffs, making their imports worth significantly more. They need to find that extra cash to maintain stock, or we might just see supply shrink, leading to increased prices. There’s also a risk of bankruptcy, where companies might liquidate stock, resulting in lower prices, contrary to inflation expectations. It’s tough times for the industry, regardless of which path we take.

**Derek Thompson**: I want to follow up on two points you made. One is about the option for toy industry players to "cheat to win." Can you explain what you mean by that? And then I’d like to discuss how uncertainty manifests in a company like yours.

**Molson Hart**: To put it simply, if tariffs exceed the cost of goods—which is entirely possible—you shift your business focus entirely. For example, let’s say we buy a product for $1, but it has an invoice value of 10 cents. Instead of a $1.45 tariff, you could face a $14.5 tariff per unit. 

One way to cheat is to inflate the value on the invoice. Another method could involve claiming production in Vietnam in a less-than-honest manner. Maybe you route a shipment through Vietnam or stamp "Made in Vietnam" on packages, even if that’s not the case. 

Moreover, these tariffs create incentive problems. In the U.S., companies are held accountable for cheating. Their misconduct can lead to penalties or even jail time. Conversely, foreign companies can cheat and only risk losing their shipment, making cheating more appealing for them than for American firms.

**Derek Thompson**: Moving a supply chain isn't as straightforward as just calling a factory and saying, “Pack up and move.” I can imagine you’ve had experience moving supply chains out of China, especially in recent years. Can you share that experience?

**Molson Hart**: Certainly. In 2020 and early 2021, container shipping costs skyrocketed—from about $4,000 to $25,000 per container. Since we sell relatively large products, that cost increase was monumental. We thought about manufacturing in Mexico to save costs. 

For instance, we planned to ship flat plush skins to Mexico, fill them there to avoid high container fees, and import the finished products into the U.S. However, getting responses from Mexican manufacturers was challenging. I even visited a specialized manufacturing town in Mexico to find solutions, but the process was frustrating. 

Ultimately, we faced a roadblock when the manufacturer couldn’t secure the import/export license necessary for the operation. The cost of the license shot up to $500,000, leading us to send back our stock of skins, which have remained in storage for years. This highlights how critical government regulations can complicate manufacturing processes.

**Derek Thompson**: Does your experience in Mexico demonstrate broader challenges that toy companies—and even those in electronics and other sectors—will face when trying to move away from China?

**Molson Hart**: Absolutely. Our attempt in Mexico wasn’t even overly complicated, yet we struggled to manage a relatively simple operation. Now, imagine trying to manufacture an entire toy from scratch in the U.S.! Where would we source the fabric or components from?

Manufacturing a complete stuffed animal is significantly harder than most people realize. When you consider high-tech products like iPhones, the challenge of bringing manufacturing back to the U.S. might be even more daunting.
