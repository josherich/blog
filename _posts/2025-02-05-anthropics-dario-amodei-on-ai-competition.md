I think it's perfectly possible to distribute the benefits of AI to the whole world, including China and other authoritarian countries, without distributing the military capabilities. The plan has to start from a position of strength and work out how this technology can benefit everyone and how its downsides can be mitigated. 

Dario Amadei, CEO of Anthropic, joins us today. Thank you for having me. Let’s start with a brief overview of how rapid AI progress could translate into national power. A few months ago, I wrote an essay called "Machines of Loving Grace," which focused on many positive applications of very powerful AI. In it, I offered a definition of what I believe very powerful AI will look like, encapsulated in the phrase “country of geniuses in a data center.” This phrase evokes a compelling understanding of the implications of powerful AI technologies. 

Imagine dropping a country of 10 million people into a data center, with all of them being polymathic Nobel Prize winners in any field. What does that do to national power? It presumably accelerates economic ability and science exponentially. Moreover, it likely has implications for intelligence and national defense, particularly for controlling swarms of drones or analyzing intelligence information. With a considerable number of incredibly intelligent entities controlling various operations virtually, that will inherently serve as a significant source of power.

Now, regarding export controls—why write an essay on this subject? In light of the reactions to DeepSeq and my involvement in the industry, I observed many inaccuracies in those reactions. These inaccuracies stemmed from people not closely following the evolving dynamics of technology. With the release of the model developed by a Chinese company, people seemed to misinterpret the situation as if they had missed many previous advancements and had landed on this breakthrough. They viewed it under an old stereotype that cheap technology comes from China. However, as I mentioned in my essay, there has been an ongoing cost decrease in this field alongside increasing investments to enhance model performance. The economic utility of these powerful models has consistently surpassed any trends toward cheaper production. 

What is significant here is that a competitor has emerged. In the landscape of companies that can train AI—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, perhaps Meta and X AI—DeepSeq is now another potential player in that category. This addition is a milestone that didn't exist before and raises important concerns. Yet, I believe many overreacted and misjudged the implications of this development. 

People should reassess the model landscape, particularly regarding the capabilities of Chinese companies. For a long time, we’ve been aware that DeepSeq is likely the most capable entity in China. While previously there were just three to five companies in the U.S. capable of creating frontier models, there is now one in China that can potentially match them. The continuous production of near frontier models will depend on their access to chips and the scale at which they can acquire them. 

The AI safety community, including yourself, has warned about the risks of racing dynamics for years. How did you arrive at the conclusion that these concerns and the opportunities provided by AI technologies are not mutually exclusive? I remain concerned about a race, particularly if the U.S. and China become equally matched in AI capabilities. In such a scenario, there would be a strong incentive for both sides to continue advancing the technology without restraint due to the immense economic and military value involved.

Legislation in the United States intended to mitigate the risks of AI systems has faced opposition based on the argument that slowing down could allow China to leap ahead. I believe this argument has merit. Therefore, the only way to navigate this is through measures like export controls, which could create a margin between the U.S. and China. If the U.S. is two years ahead, we can dedicate a portion of that time to ensuring the technologies we develop are safe while still maintaining our lead. 

The distinguishing factor in this context is that there are cooperative advantages within Western companies that don't exist in international relations with China. In the West, coordination mechanisms allow companies to adhere to shared regulations and interests, fostering shared safety practices. However, in a Hobbesian international landscape, the same cannot be said for the relationship between the U.S. and China. Opportunities for cooperation with China exist, but they remain constrained by the adversarial nature of the two systems. 

Moreover, should compelling evidence of imminent dangers from AI systems arise, it could change the dynamics of this race. So far, the arguments about the potential risks have not reached a level that would compel both countries to relinquish or pause their technological advancements in AI.

Regarding export controls, the aim should be to regulate which chips are sold to China effectively. The export controls were likely never designed to prevent DeepSeq or other Chinese companies from acquiring the chips they needed, but rather to limit the scale of their access. Nonetheless, smuggling and other tactics have likely circumvented initial export controls. 

The nuances of exports, including specific chips like the H-100 and H-800, demonstrate the complexities involved. The H-800 was specifically developed after previous export controls to circumvent restrictions, revealing how companies adapt to regulatory landscapes. The implications of these developments extend to the potential gap between the leading labs and others, depending on how well security mechanisms are set and maintained.

This dynamic should also include considerations regarding the risks posed by model distillation, where a model can be reverse-engineered. It is essential to develop both detection methods and monitoring techniques to minimize such theft. As advanced AI systems proliferate and integrate counterintelligence measures, these will become important tools in protecting valuable intellectual property, particularly in the competitive landscape dominated by powerful nation-states.

The stakes are incredibly high, and navigating the intersection of AI development and international relations will pose significant challenges. The possibility of powerful AI benefiting society at large remains, but it necessitates responsible governance and thoughtful approaches to both development and distribution on a global scale. 

In conclusion, there are substantial opportunities for AI to support fairer systems of justice and engage citizens in democratic processes. Yet it is crucial to remain cognizant of who wields this technology and how it can be manipulated by authoritarian regimes. The balance between utilizing AI for societal benefits and imposing adequate safeguards against its potential misuse will determine its ultimate impact on global governance and societal structures.
